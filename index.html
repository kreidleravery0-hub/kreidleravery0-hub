1. What AI is (and isn't)

Artificial intelligence (AI)

The broad field of building computer systems that perform tasks normally associated with human intelligence: recognizing images and speech, understanding language, making predictions, and making decisions.

Machine learning (ML)

A subset of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. Example: a spam filter trained on labeled emails.

Deep learning

A subset of ML using neural networks with many layers. Powers modern image recognition, speech recognition, and language models.

Generative AI

AI that creates new content — text, images, audio, video, code — based on patterns in its training data. Examples: chatbots, image generators.

Narrow AI vs. general AI

  • Narrow (weak) AI: built for specific tasks (translation, recommendations, face unlock). AI in use today is not general. General-purpose models like LLMs handle many tasks, but they are still not AGI.
  • Artificial general intelligence (AGI): a hypothetical system able to learn and perform any intellectual task a human can. It does not currently exist.

Predictive vs. generative AI

  • Predictive / discriminative: classifies or forecasts (Is this email spam? Will this loan default?).
  • Generative: produces new output (write an essay, draw a picture).
RememberAI you already use: search ranking, streaming and shopping recommendations, social media feeds, spam filters, voice assistants, autocorrect, navigation apps, and face unlock.

2. How machines learn

The basic pipeline

  1. Data: collect examples (text, images, numbers). Quality and representativeness matter more than almost anything else.
  2. Training: an algorithm adjusts the model's internal numbers (parameters or weights) to reduce its errors on the examples.
  3. Model: the trained result — a mathematical function that maps inputs to outputs.
  4. Testing / evaluation: measure performance on data the model has not seen.
  5. Inference: using the trained model on new inputs (e.g., you asking a chatbot a question).

Three main types of machine learning

TypeHow it learnsExample
SupervisedFrom labeled examples (input + correct answer)Photos labeled "cat"/"dog"; spam vs. not spam
UnsupervisedFinds structure in unlabeled dataGrouping customers by shopping habits (clustering)
ReinforcementTrial and error with rewards and penaltiesGame-playing AI; robot control; tuning chatbot behavior

Neural networks in one paragraph

Loosely inspired by the brain, a neural network is layers of simple connected units ("neurons"). Each connection has a weight. Input flows through the layers to produce an output; during training, the weights are nudged to make outputs closer to the correct answers. Modern models have millions to hundreds of billions or more of these weights.

Common misconception"The AI was programmed to say that." Usually no one wrote that specific response — the behavior emerged from patterns in training data and the training process. This is also why AI decisions can be hard to explain (the black box problem).

Overfitting

When a model memorizes its training data instead of learning general patterns, it does well on training examples but poorly on new data. This is why models are tested on held-out data.

3. How large language models (LLMs) work

Chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot are built on LLMs.

  1. Tokens: text is split into small chunks (words or pieces of words). Models read and write tokens, not letters or ideas.
  2. Pretraining: the model reads a huge amount of text and learns to predict the next token. From this it picks up grammar, facts, styles, and patterns of reasoning — and also errors and biases in that text.
  3. Fine-tuning and human feedback: the model is further trained to follow instructions and be helpful and safe, often using human ratings of responses (reinforcement learning from human feedback, RLHF).
  4. Generating a response: the model produces one token at a time, each chosen based on probabilities given everything before it. Some randomness (temperature) means the same prompt can give different answers.
Key ideaAn LLM generates the most plausible-sounding continuation, not a checked fact. It has no built-in way to know whether what it says is true.

Terms to know

  • Transformer: the neural network architecture behind modern LLMs (introduced 2017). Its "attention" mechanism lets the model weigh how words relate across a passage.
  • Prompt: the input or instructions you give the model.
  • Context window: how much text (in tokens) the model can consider at once. Content beyond it is not "remembered."
  • Training cutoff: the model's knowledge stops at the date its training data ends, unless it is connected to search or other tools.
  • Multimodal: models that handle more than text — images, audio, video.

Grounding, tools, and agents

  • Grounding / retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the model searches the web or a set of documents and uses the results in its answer. This reduces, but does not eliminate, hallucinations — it can still misread or misquote sources.
  • Tool use and AI agents: models connected to tools can browse, run code, edit files, or take actions (send email, make purchases) over multiple steps. Mistakes can now have real effects, so human review matters more.

4. Limitations and risks of AI output

Hallucinations

Confident but false or made-up output: wrong facts, invented quotes, fake sources and citations, nonexistent court cases or studies.

Bias

Models reproduce stereotypes and gaps in their training data. More in Ethics.

Outdated knowledge

Without live search, a model doesn't know events after its training cutoff and may not say so.

Inconsistency

The same question can get different answers; small wording changes can change results.

Reasoning and math errors

Models can make logic, counting, and arithmetic mistakes while sounding certain.

Sycophancy

A tendency to agree with the user or tell them what they want to hear, even when the user is wrong.

Exam trapSounding confident, fluent, or detailed is not evidence that an AI answer is correct.

5. Ethics and societal impact

Bias and fairness

If training data is skewed, AI can discriminate — for example, in hiring, lending, policing, or healthcare. Facial analysis systems have been shown to be less accurate for some demographic groups, especially darker-skinned women (Gender Shades study, Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). Fairness requires diverse data, testing across groups, and human oversight.

Privacy and data

  • AI is trained on massive data sets, which may include personal information collected without clear consent.
  • Prompts you enter may be stored, reviewed, or used to train future models, depending on the service's settings and policies.
  • Don't enter passwords, financial details, health information, or other people's private data.

Misinformation and deepfakes

Generative AI makes it cheap to produce realistic fake text, images, audio, and video. Deepfakes can be used for scams, impersonation, harassment, and political manipulation.

Copyright and intellectual property

Many models were trained on copyrighted work without permission, leading to lawsuits from authors, artists, and news organizations. Who owns AI-generated content is also unsettled; in the U.S., the Copyright Office has said works need human authorship to be protected.

Transparency, explainability, and accountability

  • Black box: it is often hard to explain why a model gave a specific output.
  • Accountability: when AI causes harm, responsibility lies with the people and organizations who build, deploy, and use it.
  • Human in the loop: keeping people involved in high-stakes decisions.

Regulation and governance

  • EU AI Act (adopted 2024): the first major comprehensive AI law. It sorts AI uses by risk level — banned (unacceptable risk), high-risk (strict requirements), limited-risk (transparency duties, e.g., disclosing chatbots and deepfakes), and minimal-risk.
  • United States: no single comprehensive federal AI law; rules come from a mix of existing laws, state laws, and agency guidance.
  • Institutions: schools and employers set their own AI-use policies.

Work, environment, and access

  • Jobs: AI automates some tasks, changes many jobs, and creates new ones; effects are uneven across fields.
  • Environment: training and running large models use significant electricity and water for data centers.
  • Digital divide: unequal access to AI tools and skills can widen existing inequalities.
  • Hidden labor: much AI depends on human workers who label data and review harmful content, often for low pay.

6. Using AI effectively and responsibly

Writing good prompts

  • Be specific: state the task, audience, length, and format.
  • Give context: include background or examples of what you want.
  • Assign a role when helpful ("Act as a tutor quizzing me on…").
  • Iterate: refine and follow up; treat it as a conversation.
  • Ask for reasoning or sources — then check them.

Verifying AI output

  1. Check claims against reliable sources (library databases, official sites, reputable news, textbooks).
  2. Confirm every citation actually exists and says what the AI claims.
  3. Use lateral reading: open new tabs to see what other trusted sources say.
  4. Be extra careful with numbers, dates, quotes, medical, legal, and financial information.

Academic integrity

  • Follow the AI policy for each course and assignment; policies differ.
  • Disclose how you used AI when required, and cite it in the style your instructor requires (APA, MLA, and Chicago all publish guidance for citing generative AI).
  • You are responsible for everything you submit, including AI errors.
  • AI detectors are unreliable. They produce false positives (flagging human writing as AI), especially for non-native English writers, and can be fooled. Keep drafts and notes to show your process.
  • Good uses usually include brainstorming, explaining concepts, quizzing yourself, and feedback on your own drafts. Submitting AI-written work as your own is typically not allowed.
Rule of thumbUse AI as an assistant, not an authority: you make the decisions and verify the facts.

8. Glossary

AGI
Artificial general intelligence: hypothetical AI with human-level ability across any task.
AI agent
An AI system that uses tools and takes multi-step actions toward a goal.
Algorithm
A set of step-by-step instructions for solving a problem or doing a task.
Bias (algorithmic)
Systematic, unfair outcomes from an AI system, often caused by its data or design.
Chatbot
A program that converses with users in natural language.
Context window
The maximum amount of text a model can take into account at once.
Deep learning
Machine learning using neural networks with many layers.
Deepfake
AI-generated or altered media that realistically imitates a real person.
Fine-tuning
Additional training of a pretrained model on specific data or tasks.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content such as text, images, audio, or code.
Hallucination
Confident AI output that is false or fabricated.
Inference
Using a trained model to produce outputs from new inputs.
LLM
Large language model: a model trained on vast text to predict and generate language.
Machine learning (ML)
AI that learns patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules.
Natural language processing (NLP)
The AI field focused on understanding and generating human language.
Neural network
Layers of connected units with adjustable weights that learn from data.
Overfitting
When a model memorizes training data and performs poorly on new data.
Parameters (weights)
The internal numbers a model adjusts during training.
Prompt
The input or instructions given to an AI model.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: the model looks up documents or search results and uses them in its answer.
RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: training a model using human ratings of its responses.
Supervised learning
Learning from labeled examples that include the correct answer.
Temperature
A setting controlling how random a model's word choices are; higher means more varied output.
Token
A chunk of text (word or word piece) that a language model processes.
Training cutoff
The date after which a model's training data ends.
Training data
The examples a model learns from.
Transformer
The neural network architecture behind modern LLMs, based on "attention."
Unsupervised learning
Finding patterns in data that has no labels.

9. Self-test

Answer out loud or on paper first, then open each question to check.

What is the relationship between AI, machine learning, and deep learning?

They are nested: deep learning is a type of machine learning, which is a type of AI. Most generative AI, and all LLMs, are built with deep learning.

What is the difference between supervised and unsupervised learning?

Supervised learning uses labeled examples with correct answers; unsupervised learning finds patterns in unlabeled data.

At its core, what task is an LLM trained to do?

Predict the next token in a sequence of text.

What is a hallucination, and why does it happen?

Confident output that is false or made up. It happens because LLMs generate plausible-sounding text from patterns rather than looking up verified facts.

Give two ways bias can enter an AI system.

Unrepresentative or historically biased training data; design choices such as what the model optimizes for or how labels were assigned; lack of testing across different groups.

Why might a chatbot give wrong answers about recent events?

Its knowledge stops at its training cutoff unless it is connected to live search.

Is today's AI "narrow" or "general"?

Narrow (not general). General-purpose models like LLMs handle many tasks, but AGI is hypothetical and does not currently exist.

Name three steps for checking an AI-generated answer.

Verify claims in reliable sources; confirm citations exist and say what is claimed; use lateral reading; be extra careful with numbers, quotes, and high-stakes topics.

Why shouldn't an AI detector's result be treated as proof of cheating?

Detectors are unreliable: they produce false positives, especially for non-native English writers, and can be fooled.

What should you avoid typing into a public AI tool?

Passwords, financial or health information, and other people's private or confidential data.

Who is responsible when AI-generated content in your assignment is wrong?

You are. The person who submits or acts on AI output is accountable for it.
